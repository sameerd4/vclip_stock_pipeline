## Part I — VClip Legal Philosophy

### 1. Purpose

VClip licenses independently created stock footage, primarily place-based aerial and ground video, for incorporation into customer productions. The legal model should be easy for ordinary customers to understand while remaining credible to professional buyers, agencies, broadcasters, and film studios.

The central principle is simple:

**VClip licenses the copyright and usage rights that VClip owns or controls in the footage. VClip does not automatically grant or warrant every separate third-party right that may be visible, audible, or implicated by a customer's particular downstream use.**

That principle should be stated clearly before purchase, reflected in item metadata, and repeated in the license agreement.

### 2. What VClip should stand behind

VClip should make affirmative promises only about matters it can reasonably control and verify. Subject to counsel review, those promises should include:

- VClip owns, or has sufficient authority to license, the copyright in the licensed footage.
- The delivered file is the file identified by the applicable license/order and materially matches the listed technical specifications.
- VClip has not knowingly misrepresented whether a model release, property release, or other clearance is on file.
- VClip performs a documented internal review designed to identify material rights issues before footage is offered for sale.
- VClip does not knowingly offer footage that it has classified as blocked or not distributable.

VClip should avoid guaranteeing facts that depend on a customer's later use, such as whether a depicted trademark can be used in a national advertising campaign, whether a broadcaster's insurer will approve a shot, or whether a foreign jurisdiction imposes a clearance rule that does not apply in the United States.

### 3. The footage and the things depicted in the footage are different rights layers

A VClip video is itself a copyrighted audiovisual work. Separate rights may exist in things that happen to appear in the frame, including:

- trademarks, logos, trade dress, and brand names;
- copyrighted artwork, murals, sculptures, graphics, and screen content;
- names, likenesses, privacy rights, and publicity rights of identifiable people;
- performances, sporting events, concerts, or broadcasts;
- private-property access or contractual filming restrictions;
- music, dialogue, or ambient audio;
- other jurisdiction-specific rights.

The customer receives the rights VClip expressly licenses in the footage. Unless an item is expressly identified as having a particular release or clearance on file, the customer should not assume that VClip is granting rights owned by third parties merely because those third-party elements appear in a frame.

This allocation is consistent with the way established stock licensors address depicted third-party intellectual property. For example, Pond5's current license expressly separates its license in the content from warranties concerning buildings, trademarks, trade dress, logos, art, architecture, and other depicted works. [S4]

### 4. Marketplace moderation rules are not the same thing as the law

VClip should distinguish legal analysis from the submission policies of third-party stock marketplaces.

For example, Adobe Stock currently imposes conservative contributor requirements for recognizable private property, certain landmarks, modern architecture, logos, and other protected elements. Those rules are important if VClip submits content to Adobe, but they should not automatically be treated as a statement that every such depiction is unlawful to license directly. [S6]

U.S. copyright law itself contains a specific limitation for constructed architectural works: copyright in an architectural work does not include the right to prevent pictures or other pictorial representations of a building when the building is located in or ordinarily visible from a public place. [S1]

Likewise, trademark law focuses heavily on confusion about source, affiliation, sponsorship, or approval. The mere appearance of a trademark in an independently created scene is not the same thing as falsely suggesting that the trademark owner sponsored the customer's production. [S2]

VClip's review process should therefore identify and disclose material third-party elements without reflexively treating every recognizable building or incidental logo as forbidden footage.

### 5. People and publicity rights

VClip's ordinary landscape and aerial catalog should avoid intentionally identifying private individuals. When people appear, the rights review should focus on whether a person is actually recognizable or singled out, not simply whether human figures exist somewhere in a wide shot.

Publicity and privacy rules vary by jurisdiction. As one concrete example, California Civil Code section 3344 addresses commercial uses of a person's name, voice, photograph, or likeness and defines a person in a photograph as readily identifiable when a viewer can reasonably determine who the depicted person is. The statute also distinguishes people represented merely as members of a definable group from individuals who are singled out. [S3]

VClip should not present that California rule as universal. Instead, it should support a conservative internal flagging process and require model releases where VClip affirmatively markets recognizable individuals for ordinary commercial stock use.

### 6. Trademarks and recognizable properties

VClip should not promise that every visible trademark or recognizable property has been affirmatively cleared for every possible use.

Instead, VClip should distinguish between:

- **incidental depiction** — a mark, sign, building, vehicle, or brand happens to be visible as part of a larger scene;
- **prominent depiction** — the third-party element is a major or dominant subject of the shot;
- **endorsement-sensitive use** — the buyer's proposed use could reasonably imply sponsorship, affiliation, approval, or a commercial relationship with the depicted rights holder;
- **restricted content** — footage of an event, performance, interior, location, or subject for which VClip knows a separate restriction applies.

The customer license should prohibit false or misleading endorsement and make the customer responsible for additional permissions triggered by the customer's particular use.

### 7. Recommended VClip distribution classifications

VClip should use simple internal/customer-visible classifications that describe distribution posture without pretending to issue a legal opinion.

| Classification | Meaning | Customer treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **STANDARD** | No material third-party issue identified in VClip's review. | Licensed under the ordinary commercial license. |
| **STANDARD WITH NOTICE** | Footage is licensable, but identifiable third-party property, branding, artwork, or another element is present and no blanket third-party clearance is included. | Licensed commercially, subject to the depicted-rights notice and no-endorsement rule. |
| **EDITORIAL ONLY** | VClip has determined that ordinary promotional/advertising use should not be offered without additional rights. | Limited to bona fide editorial, documentary, news, public-affairs, or other permitted non-promotional uses stated in the license. |
| **NEEDS CLEARANCE** | VClip has identified a material issue requiring additional research, a release, or a specific license before distribution. | Not offered under the standard store license. |
| **BLOCKED** | Known issue makes distribution inappropriate or unauthorized. | Not offered. |

These labels should be treated as VClip's distribution policy, not as a representation that a court, platform, insurer, or rights holder will reach the same conclusion for every use.

### 8. Example: San Francisco skyline footage containing Oracle Park

For a lawfully captured exterior establishing shot where Oracle Park appears as part of a broader San Francisco skyline, no live professional sporting event is shown, no identifiable person is being exploited, and Giants/Oracle branding is incidental rather than the dominant subject, VClip's working policy would generally be **STANDARD WITH NOTICE**, not automatically **EDITORIAL ONLY** or **BLOCKED**.

The notice would tell the buyer that third-party trademarks/property may be depicted and that the license does not grant Giants, MLB, Oracle, or other third-party trademark/property rights. The buyer may incorporate the footage into a film, real-estate reel, event recap, corporate video, or other production permitted by the license, but may not use the footage in a way that falsely suggests sponsorship or endorsement and remains responsible for any additional clearance its specific context requires.

A tight shot whose primary commercial value is a clearly visible Giants or Oracle mark may warrant a different classification. Footage of a live professional sporting event is outside VClip's ordinary stock policy and should be separately excluded or reviewed.

This example is a product-policy illustration, not a legal opinion about Oracle Park or any particular rights holder.

### 9. Customer responsibility should be use-specific

A stock-footage seller cannot know every context in which a buyer will use a clip. The same San Francisco establishing shot might appear in:

- a wedding recap;
- a birthday or travel reel;
- a real-estate listing video;
- a tourism advertisement;
- a documentary;
- a feature film;
- a political advertisement;
- an advertisement criticizing a depicted company;
- merchandise or packaging.

Those contexts do not carry identical legal risks. VClip should therefore license broad production use while placing responsibility for context-specific clearances on the customer.

The customer should expressly agree not to use footage in a way that is unlawful, defamatory, deceptive, or likely to create a false impression of endorsement, affiliation, sponsorship, or approval by a depicted person or organization.

### 10. Customer protections matter too

The license should not be drafted as a one-sided disclaimer. A professional buyer needs confidence that VClip is actually entitled to license the file.

VClip should therefore stand behind a narrow but meaningful core warranty: VClip owns or controls sufficient rights in the footage itself to grant the stated license. If a third-party claim arises solely because VClip did not own the footage it purported to license, VClip should provide a defined remedy and limited indemnity, subject to reasonable claim procedures and a monetary cap.

By contrast, VClip should not indemnify a buyer for a claim caused by:

- the buyer's modification of the footage;
- a misleading caption, voiceover, juxtaposition, or advertisement created by the buyer;
- a use that implies endorsement or affiliation;
- failure to obtain a clearance specifically required by the buyer's use;
- use outside the permitted license scope;
- depicted third-party rights that the item page/license expressly says are not included.

Major stock licensors similarly allocate risk through defined warranties, exclusions for use context, customer indemnities, and liability caps rather than warranting every conceivable downstream use. [S4] [S5]

### 11. Enterprise buyers should be able to buy more certainty

A film studio, broadcaster, agency, or major brand may need more than the standard license provides. VClip should eventually support an enterprise clearance workflow that may include:

- enhanced manual rights review;
- a documented chain of title;
- copies of model/property releases actually on file;
- provenance and capture records;
- an item-specific clearance memorandum;
- negotiated representations and warranties;
- higher indemnity limits;
- errors-and-omissions insurance where commercially justified.

The existence of an enterprise path should not force VClip to over-warrant every $20-$100 consumer/creator license.

### 12. Claim-handling philosophy

If VClip learns of a credible third-party claim concerning licensed footage, it should have the contractual right to investigate and, where appropriate:

1. confirm that the customer's use is within the license;
2. obtain or document additional rights;
3. offer substitute footage;
4. refund the applicable license fee;
5. require the customer to stop future uses of the raw clip or affected production where legally necessary;
6. coordinate defense of a covered claim under any applicable indemnity.

The agreement should avoid requiring recall of already distributed productions except where required by law, court order, settlement, or a separately negotiated enterprise agreement.

With ordinary commercial VClip footage, you can incorporate the clip into a finished project such as a film, ad, social video, real-estate reel, wedding/event recap, corporate video, website, documentary, or presentation. You may edit and color-grade the footage and deliver the finished project to your client.

Your license is worldwide and perpetual for the licensed Production, subject to the order and the restrictions below.

## What you cannot do

You cannot resell the raw VClip file, upload it to another stock library, give the raw file to someone for unrelated reuse, use it as your logo, use it unlawfully or deceptively, or use it to falsely imply that a person, brand, venue, team, or other depicted third party endorses you or your client.

## What VClip promises

VClip promises that it owns or controls sufficient rights in the footage itself to give you the license stated in your order, subject to the full agreement. If that core ownership promise is wrong and a covered claim results, the agreement provides a defined remedy and limited indemnity.

## What VClip does not automatically promise

A video can lawfully belong to VClip while separately depicting a trademark, building, artwork, person, or other third-party element. Unless the item specifically says a release or clearance is on file, your VClip license does not automatically include every separate third-party right visible in the scene.

Whether you need another clearance can depend on how you use the clip. A skyline shot used as an establishing shot in a film is different from using the same image in an advertisement that implies a depicted company sponsors the product.

## What "Standard With Notice" means

It means VClip considers the clip licensable under the ordinary license but has identified a potentially relevant third-party element. You may use the clip under the license, but VClip is not promising that the depicted third party has approved every possible downstream use.

## If you are a studio, broadcaster, agency, or major brand

Contact VClip for an Enterprise review if you need additional chain-of-title documentation, release copies, a higher indemnity limit, an item-specific clearance review, exclusivity, or other negotiated protections.

# Part IV — Attorney Review Checklist

Before launch, counsel should specifically review and resolve the following:

1. **Legal entity and jurisdiction.** Confirm the entity granting licenses and governing-law/venue provisions.
2. **License scope.** Decide whether the standard purchase is one Production, unlimited Productions, or another model; define seats and contractor access.
3. **Indemnity cap.** Select a commercially appropriate standard indemnity cap and enterprise tiers.
4. **General liability cap.** Decide whether it equals the license fee, a fixed amount, or another formula.
5. **Depicted-rights clause.** Confirm the scope and enforceability of the disclaimer for trademarks, trade dress, property, artwork, publicity rights, and other depicted rights.
6. **Model/property release policy.** Establish when VClip will require releases, how releases are stored, and what customer-facing promises are made when a release is marked "on file."
7. **Capture legality representation.** Decide whether VClip should warrant lawful capture, warrant only that it has no known capture violation, or treat capture compliance as internal provenance rather than a customer warranty.
8. **Right of publicity.** Review state-law exposure and any rules for identifiable people, sensitive uses, crowds, and public events.
9. **Architecture/art distinction.** Preserve the U.S. architectural-work rule while separately handling murals, sculptures, screen content, and other copyrighted works.
10. **Trademark / false endorsement.** Confirm the no-endorsement restrictions and how prominent branded subjects should be classified.
11. **Editorial classification.** Define what VClip will and will not sell as EDITORIAL ONLY and whether credits are required.
12. **Claims and cease-use.** Confirm notice periods, defense control, replacement/refund remedies, and treatment of previously distributed Productions.
13. **E&O insurance.** Determine whether and when VClip should maintain media liability/errors-and-omissions coverage, and whether enterprise customers can purchase higher insured limits.
14. **Consumer versus business buyers.** Confirm whether consumer-law rules require different disclaimers, refund rights, or dispute language.
15. **International sales.** Add jurisdiction-specific terms if VClip actively markets outside the United States.
16. **AI/ML restriction.** Confirm whether VClip wants to prohibit training, computer-vision, and dataset use under the standard license or create a separate data license.
17. **Privacy policy / website terms.** Keep this content license separate from VClip's Terms of Use, Privacy Policy, DMCA/IP complaint process, and payment terms.
18. **Enterprise contracting.** Create a short enterprise addendum rather than rewriting the standard license for each professional customer.

# Part V — Research Notes and Sources

These sources informed the business/legal allocation in this draft. They are references for counsel and product design, not incorporated contract terms.

**[S1] U.S. Copyright Act, 17 U.S.C. § 120 — Scope of exclusive rights in architectural works.**  
U.S. Copyright Office, Circular 92 / Title 17, Chapter 1. Section 120(a) provides that copyright in a constructed architectural work does not include the right to prevent making, distributing, or publicly displaying pictorial representations of the work when the building is located in or ordinarily visible from a public place.  
https://www.copyright.gov/title17/92chap1.html

**[S2] Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1125(a).**  
The statute addresses, among other things, uses likely to cause confusion about affiliation, connection, association, origin, sponsorship, or approval. This supports the draft's emphasis on false endorsement/affiliation rather than treating every incidental trademark depiction as automatically forbidden.  
https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/1125

**[S3] California Civil Code § 3344.**  
California's statutory right-of-publicity provision addresses knowing commercial use of another person's name, voice, signature, photograph, or likeness and defines a person as readily identifiable in a photograph when a viewer can reasonably determine who the depicted person is. The law varies by state; this is included as a concrete example rather than a universal rule.  
https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=3344.

**[S4] Pond5 Content License Agreement, current page reviewed August 20, 2026 (page states revised January 3, 2024).**  
Relevant structure includes a broad production license, restrictions on raw redistribution, a dedicated "Depicted IP" section excluding licenses/warranties for certain buildings, trademarks, logos, art, architecture and other depicted works absent specified releases, editorial-use rules, claim procedures, warranties, indemnities, and liability caps. VClip should not copy Pond5's text; this source is a benchmark for risk allocation.  
https://www.pond5.com/legal/license

**[S5] Shutterstock Terms of Service & License Agreements, current page reviewed August 20, 2026.**  
Relevant structure includes restrictions against third-party trademark infringement and deceptive use, a prohibition on AI-training use of licensed visual content, express contributor-rights warranties, indemnity tied to express warranties, exclusions for customer modifications/context, and license-specific limits of liability.

## Proposed implementation principle for VClip

The software should preserve the distinction embodied in this draft:

**Evidence → Review → Classification → Customer Notice → License**

- **Evidence:** provenance, technical metadata, visual observations, releases, known restrictions.
- **Review:** human determination of what is materially depicted and whether a special issue exists.
- **Classification:** STANDARD, STANDARD WITH NOTICE, EDITORIAL ONLY, NEEDS CLEARANCE, or BLOCKED.
- **Customer Notice:** clear item-level disclosure of any material limitation or missing third-party clearance.
- **License:** rights in VClip's footage plus clearly allocated responsibilities for the customer's particular use.

The system should never convert "AI did not notice a logo/person" into a legal clearance. AI may assist discovery; legal classification remains a policy/human-review function.
